# Harvest timing separates silage from grain corn

The answer is when and how a field loses its canopy. Silage corn is chopped green, at 60-70% whole-plant moisture, weeks before grain corn is combined. In the Upper Midwest that leaves a window from about mid-September to mid-October when **silage fields are already bare and grain fields are still standing and brown**. A per-field harvest-date detector run on Sentinel-2/HLS, with Sentinel-1 filling in under cloud, captures most of the signal. A post-harvest residue check is a second cue: grain harvest leaves stover and silage harvest leaves almost bare soil. Only one peer-reviewed study maps silage and grain maize as separate classes. Lin et al. 2025 (Hohhot, China) used a three-rule Sentinel-2 decision tree on September LSWI and reached **OA 0.929 (kappa 0.892)**. Thresholds had to be re-tuned by hand for other years ([PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/agronomy/agronomy-15-01438/article_deploy/agronomy-15-01438.pdf)). We found no US study, and the USDA Cropland Data Layer (CDL) has a single Corn class with no silage split ([CDL FAQ](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Research_and_Science/Cropland/sarsfaqs2.php)). That makes the gap real and the pitch easy: a silage/grain layer on top of CDL, validated against NASS county silage acreage. Labels are the hardest part, not the model. Plan the 22 hours around a rule-based harvest-timing baseline, random forest (RF) or gradient boosting on pseudo-labels, and county-level validation. Deep models and a Prithvi fine-tune are not worth the build time.

## 1. One direct benchmark exists, and it is Chinese, not American

**Lin, Huang, Tan, Yang, Huang et al. 2025, "Silage Maize Identification Using a Temporal Difference-Based Model with Sentinel-2 Data" (Agronomy 15:1438)** is the only clear three-class study (silage / grain / other) the research found ([PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/agronomy/agronomy-15-01438/article_deploy/agronomy-15-01438.pdf)). Hohhot has one crop cycle per year. Silage is harvested from mid-August to early September at late milk to early dough stage, and grain from late September to early October. In 2023 silage was "fully harvested by 10 September" (same PDF). The method uses 15-day Sentinel-2 NDVI and LSWI composites in GEE, Whittaker-smoothed, with three rules. A maize mask is NDVI_May ≤ 0.2, NDVI_July ≥ 0.2 and elevation < 1200 m. A July canopy check is LSWI_July ≥ 0.16. The deciding rule is **LSWI_Sept ≤ 0.15 → silage, otherwise grain**. Results were silage UA/PA/F1 0.922/0.94/0.931 and grain 0.922/0.901/0.911. An RF baseline reached OA 0.904. Gini importance ranked **LSWI > NDVI > RENDVI > EVI**. Transfer to 2022 and 2021 gave OA 0.862 and 0.882, and only after "adaptively adjusting decision thresholds". The authors name manual threshold tuning as a limit and suggest Sentinel-1 for cloudy regions (same PDF). One trap: the abstract lists "0.9043 vs. 0.9291" in an order that makes RF look better. The body tables confirm TempDiff = 0.9291 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy15061438)).

Other papers with "silage maize" in the title mostly separate maize from non-maize. Salehi Shahrabi et al. 2020 (IJRS) detected silage maize from Sentinel-2 NDVI using the slope from peak greenness to harvest divided by season length. They reported kappa 0.8-0.9 at three Iranian sites and in **Tulare County, California** in 2017 ([abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01431161.2020.1779377)). The abstract frames the task as silage maize versus other crops. The full text was paywalled, so whether it contrasts silage with grain is **[UNVERIFIED]**. Ruf et al. 2021 mapped maize in western Germany and assumed it was silage because of biogas demand ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/land10020128)). The research found **no US study, no EU study using LPIS (EU farmer parcel declarations) silage/grain codes as two classes, and no IGARSS paper** on the topic, across searches in English, French, German and Dutch.

Harvest-date detection is the building block, and it is mature. **NHPI** (Liu et al. 2025, RSE) combines NIR and NDVI from Landsat and Sentinel-2 in GEE for Midwest corn and soy. It reached a field-level MAE of 4 days (R² 0.85) and a state-level MAE of 3 days against USDA Crop Progress ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2025.115016)). NHPI relies on NIR separating senesced standing crop from residue. Silage leaves bare soil, so how NHPI behaves on silage fields is untested (inference). Sentinel-1 harvest detection is weaker:

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| Serbia, maize | Harvested/unharvested 83.4%; harvest-date MAE 6.8 days, all crops ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25072239)) |
| Ukraine, corn | Harvest-date RMSE 12.75 days ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01431161.2026.2612904)) |
| Canada | Harvest date correct only 56% of the time, mostly because of rain ([PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/remotesensing/remotesensing-12-01551/article_deploy/remotesensing-12-01551.pdf)) |
| Argentina, coherence plus VH | Harvest-detection accuracy above 93% over 80 fields ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01431161.2021.1957176)) |

## 2. The best signal is "green canopy gone before October", and residue confirms it

The agronomy makes the timing gap reliable. Wisconsin silage harvest starts about **42-47 days after silking**. Half milk line falls about **13 days before black layer**, and grain then dries in the field long after black layer ([UW A029](https://corn.agronomy.wisc.edu/AA/A029.aspx); [Penn State](https://extension.psu.edu/corn-silage-production-and-management)). NASS Crop Progress shows the gap directly. In Minnesota on Oct 3, 2022, **silage was 85% harvested and grain 5%**. On Oct 6, 2024 it was **92% vs 15%** ([MN 10-03-22](https://data-origin.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Minnesota/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/2022/MN-Crop-Progress-10-03-22.pdf); [MN 10-07-24](https://data-origin.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Minnesota/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/2024/MN-Crop-Progress-10-07-24.pdf)). Wisconsin silage was 9% harvested on Sept 8, 2024 and 67% by Sept 29 ([WI 09-30-24](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Wisconsin/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/2024/WI-Crop-Progress-09-30-24.pdf)). Iowa grain was only 22% harvested on Oct 7, 2024 ([IA 10-07-24](https://publications.iowa.gov/50810/1/IA-Crop-Progress-10-07-24.pdf)). The Crop Progress figures came from search excerpts of the PDFs. For 2026, the Wisconsin narrative and table text conflicted, so check the PDF.

Here is how each signal ranks, with what is proven and what is inferred:

| Signal | What separates the classes | Best window (Midwest) | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Harvest date** (abrupt NDVI/NIR drop; S1 VH drop plus coherence rise) | Silage drops before about Oct 1, grain later | Late Aug-Oct | Strong. Lin 2025 and NHPI; S1 harvest work cited above |
| **Pre-cut state** (NDVI, LSWI/NDMI in the last scene before the drop) | Silage is cut green and wet; grain is senesced and dry for weeks | Sept | Lin 2025 found LSWI most important. The NDMI version is inference with no values published |
| **Post-harvest residue** (NDTI = SWIR1 vs SWIR2) | Grain leaves about 90-95% residue cover; silage is "low-residue" ([MSU](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/measure_residue_cover_to_protect_the_soil); [UW](https://extension.soils.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/68/2016/07/Hendrickson.pdf)) | First dry, clear scene after harvest, before tillage | Indirect. **No paper applies NDTI to silage vs grain** |
| **CAI / SINDRI** | More accurate residue indices | — | Need narrow SWIR bands (WorldView-3, hyperspectral). Not on Landsat or S2 ([Dennison 2023](https://content.csbs.utah.edu/~pdennison/reprints/denn/2023_Dennison_etal_RSE.pdf)) |
| **Sentinel-1 SAR** | Expected sharper VH drop when a green canopy is cut (inference). Coherence rises after harvest ([Nasirzadeh 2021](https://web.itu.edu.tr/~cakirz/papers/nasirzadeh_etal_2021.pdf)) | Cloudy Sept-Nov | Good for timing to about a week. Rain is the main error source |
| **Thermal / red-edge** | Bare soil warmer than residue (plausible). Red-edge sharpens green vs senesced | — | **Unsourced.** Optional extra features only |

NDTI has known weaknesses. Dennison et al. call it a "de facto standard", but its relationship to residue varies by site. Landsat and Sentinel-2 SWIR2 sits poorly relative to the lignocellulose absorption features ([Dennison 2023](https://content.csbs.utah.edu/~pdennison/reprints/denn/2023_Dennison_etal_RSE.pdf)). Moisture depresses it, and moisture correction cut residue RMSE from 22.7% to 4.7% (search-excerpt figures, verify: [NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014116/downloads/remotesensing-14-06128-v3.pdf)). The minimum NDTI across several dates is more reliable than a single scene ([NV5 case study](https://www.nv5geospatialsoftware.com/Learn/Case-Studies/Case-Studies-Detail/mapping-crop-residue-with-landsat-data-using-envi-for-arcgis174)). The silage-vs-grain contrast (bare vs about 90% cover) is far larger than the residue classes those studies struggled with. Even crude NDTI on a dry date should therefore separate the classes (inference).

## 4. No public US field labels exist; validate against county statistics

CDL training labels come from FSA Common Land Unit and 578 records, and those records do carry intended use: **GR (grain) vs SG (silage)**. Access is "limited to FSA and Agency partnerships", so field-level silage truth exists inside USDA but is not public ([CDL FAQ](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Research_and_Science/Cropland/sarsfaqs2.php); [FSA intended-use codes](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/sites/default/files/documents/intended_use_codes.pdf)). This is a strong USDA-mission talking point: the method could be trained on internal FSA data the team cannot see. Public US silage data exist only as aggregates. NASS county estimates cover "**6 states for corn for silage**" (June 2025) ([rcceqm25](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Methodology_and_Data_Quality/County_Estimates_Row_Crops/06_2025/rcceqm25.pdf)). The Census of Agriculture covers all counties every 5 years, with (D) suppression (suppression codes [UNVERIFIED]). FSA county crop-acreage spreadsheets exist, but whether they split corn by GR/SG is **unverified** because the page timed out ([FSA crop acreage](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/tools/informational/freedom-information-act-foia/electronic-reading-room/frequently-requested/crop-acreage-data)). Check this first: if the spreadsheets do split corn by intended use, they give county silage acres for every state. NASS counts fields planned for grain but chopped for silage as silage ([NASS](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Education_and_Outreach/Understanding_Statistics/Corn%20Acreage%20Inclusions.pdf)).

Real field-level labels exist in Europe. The **Netherlands BRP** (Dutch farmer parcel registry) codes **259 "Mais, snij-" (silage) vs 316 "Mais, korrel-" (grain)**, published with polygons each year ([RVO 259](https://www.rvo.nl/gewascodes/259); [RVO 316](https://www.rvo.nl/gewascodes/316)). Agricultural SandboxNL already provides Sentinel-1 parcel time series for about 770k BRP parcels ([Sci. Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01474-4)). France has required a précision of "001 Récolte en grains" vs "002 Récolte ensilage" since 2023. Whether the public RPG (French parcel registry) exposes that field is unverified ([DRAAF note](https://draaf.pays-de-la-loire.agriculture.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/20230102_note_codes_culture.pdf)). EuroCrops maps green_silo_maize vs grain_maize, but it puts "Corn (biogas)" and "GREEN CORN" under *grain*, so treat it as noisy ([EuroCrops CSV](https://zenodo.org/records/13951976/files/datasets_lookup-table%20-%20EuroCrops.csv?download=1)). Use the Dutch labels to show the *feature* separates the classes, not to ship a model to the US. Climate, hybrids and calendars differ (inference).

Without US labels, three cheap checks hold up. First, regress per-county predicted silage share against NASS silage/(silage + grain) harvested acres, on held-out counties. No published silage study has done this, so it is novel. Second, compare the distribution of predicted harvest dates against the Crop Progress silage and grain curves, which is how NHPI was validated ([Liu 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2025.115016)). Third, visually interpret false-colour time series, as Lin et al. did ([PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/agronomy/agronomy-15-01438/article_deploy/agronomy-15-01438.pdf)). Using dairy proximity as a weak label is **untested** in the literature.

## 5. Rules plus RF win the day; foundation models are side dishes

Deep models buy little. On BreizhCrops (a Sentinel-2 crop benchmark), the best Transformer and LSTM reach **0.80-0.81 OA vs RF 0.77-0.78**, and several CNNs did no better than RF ([ar5iv 1905.11893](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.11893)). NASS itself switched the 2025 CDL to **random forest in GEE** on 10-day Sentinel-2 + Landsat composites ([CDL FAQ](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Research_and_Science/Cropland/sarsfaqs2.php)). An RF here runs on the same stack the agency uses, which helps the feasibility score. Prithvi-EO-1.0-100M crop classification is a 13-class CDL segmenter. It scores **60.6% overall and 65.5% on corn**, using three HLS dates between March and September ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-1.0-100M-multi-temporal-crop-classification); [dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ibm-nasa-geospatial/multi-temporal-crop-classification)). That window misses the August-October harvest that carries the signal. A 2026 preprint reports Prithvi collapsing when the window misses local phenology (snippet only: [alphaxiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.08810v1)).

AlphaEarth embeddings are the cheap innovation component. They are 64-band annual vectors in GEE, designed for kNN or linear probes with few labels ([GEE](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL); [arXiv 2507.22291](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.22291v1)). It is **unproven** whether an annual summary keeps a two-to-four-week harvest-timing difference. Comparing phenology features, embeddings, and both together on the same labels makes a good slide. For weak supervision, learning from label proportions using government regional statistics has been done for Brazilian crops ([La Rosa et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.11607)). No one has done it with US NASS county data.

| Approach | Build time | Expected accuracy | Labels needed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rule: harvest date + greenness at cut + residue | 2-4 h | Lin 2025: OA 0.93 in one region with tuned thresholds | None | **Core baseline** |
| RF / LightGBM on phenology features | +2-3 h | A few points below deep models on benchmarks | Rule pseudo-labels | **Core model** |
| County-share threshold calibration to NASS | +2-3 h | Improves area agreement; pixel accuracy unknown | County statistics | **Do it** (USDA fit) |
| AlphaEarth + RF/kNN | +1-2 h | Unknown for silage | Few | **Add-on if year ≤ 2024** |
| Deep time-series models | 4-8 h + GPU | +2-4 OA points | Many | Skip |
| Prithvi fine-tune | 6-12 h + GPU | Weak baseline, wrong window | Many | Skip; mention as future work |

Build times and the verdict column are the research team's judgment, not published results.

## 6. Wisconsin is the balanced test bed, California is nearly all silage, the Corn Belt is a needle hunt

From the NASS Crop Production 2025 Summary, silage as a share of harvested corn is **California 88%, Wisconsin 22% (885k acres, the largest silage area of any state), Minnesota 4.1%, Nebraska 2.0%, Iowa 1.9%, Illinois 0.8%, US 6.4%** ([cropan26](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/795725/cropan26.txt)). **Wisconsin** has enough of both classes to train and test on, and a state Crop Progress silage series for the calendar prior ([WI annual 2024](https://nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Wisconsin/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/2024/WI-Annual-Crop-Progress-2024.pdf)). Its fall is the cloudiest and its fields the smallest (both [UNVERIFIED]), so use Sentinel-2 at 10 m and Sentinel-1. Marathon County led 2023 silage production ([WI thematic maps](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/Wisconsin/Publications/County_Estimates/2024/WI-Corn-Thematic-Maps-2023.pdf)). The other top counties named in the notes are [UNVERIFIED]. **California's San Joaquin Valley** double-crops: winter forage is cut in April-May, corn is planted in May and "usually harvested in August", then winter grain goes in during Nov-Dec ([UC cost study](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20090116063417/http:/coststudies.ucdavis.edu/files/cornsilagevs08_3.pdf); [CA Crop Progress Aug 2024](https://nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/California/Publications/Crop_Progress_&_Condition/2024/ca_20240819.pdf)). Midwest date thresholds fail there. With an 88% prior, the harder problems are corn vs sorghum silage and the fall green-up. Fall skies there are mostly clear [UNVERIFIED]. In the **Corn Belt** (Iowa, Illinois), silage is 1-2% of corn. Accuracy is meaningless at that imbalance, so report precision and recall for silage, and focus on dairy districts.

## 7. What we'd do in 22 hours, and the traps to avoid

**Plan (inference, built from the findings above).** In hours 0-2, pick **Wisconsin, 2024**. CDL, HLS, S1 and AlphaEarth all cover it, and county silage acres exist. Get a Quick Stats key, pull county silage and grain harvested acres, and check whether the FSA county files split GR/SG. Use CSB polygons, or CDL corn segments, as field units. In hours 2-8, build per-field HLS/S2 NDVI, LSWI/NDMI and NDTI series from August to November, smoothed, plus S1 VH, VH/VV and, if feasible, coherence. Extract the harvest date, the pre-cut greenness and moisture, and the minimum NDTI after harvest. Apply a Lin-style rule with the window shifted to the local Crop Progress calendar. In hours 8-14, take high-confidence rule labels as pseudo-labels and train RF in GEE, or LightGBM locally for SHAP plots. Calibrate the threshold so predicted silage share matches NASS on half the counties. Score the other half. In hours 14-18, validate the feature on a Dutch BRP 259/316 sample. Add an AlphaEarth comparison if time allows. Spend hours 18-22 on the deck, which is 40% of the score. Show a map, a county-agreement scatter, field time-series exemplars of silage vs grain, and the line "a silage layer for the CDL, trainable on FSA's internal GR/SG records".

**Pitfalls to avoid.** Do not hard-code Lin's thresholds: they needed re-tuning even within one region. Do not use random pixel splits. Split by field at minimum and by county to be credible, because random splits inflate accuracy ([Meyer 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07805)). Never score county agreement on the same counties used for calibration, or the result is circular. Expect specific confusions:

| Confusion | Effect |
|---|---|
| Moldboard plowing | Leaves 0-10% residue, so a plowed grain field looks like silage ([NRCS](https://efotg.sc.egov.usda.gov/references/Delete/2006-11-18/residuemgt%231TechNote.pdf)) |
| Stover baling | Removes about 56% of residue but still leaves more than silage ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/322395724.pdf)) |
| High-moisture corn, earlage, snaplage | Harvested earlier than dry grain but leave stover ([UW L059](https://corn.agronomy.wisc.edu/Management/L059.aspx)). Prevalence is unquantified |
| Drought-stressed corn | Dries fast, so silage looks senesced ([ISU](https://crops.extension.iastate.edu/cropnews/2020/08/silage-harvest-drought-stressed-and-severely-lodged-corn)) |
| Cover crops and California double crops | Fall green-up raises NDVI and depresses NDTI |

Use the first clear dry scene after harvest, and pair it with harvest timing rather than trusting residue alone. Sentinel-1 timing is about one week at best and rain degrades it.

## Conclusion

The literature changes the problem. This is not a hard pattern-recognition task; it is a timing problem with a labels problem attached. One satellite-visible event, the green canopy vanishing weeks before grain harvest, carries most of the information, and simple models capture it. The open question, and the innovation opportunity, is validation without field truth. Calibrating to NASS county silage acreage, and checking on held-out counties, is new in the literature and aligns with how USDA actually works.

The most valuable pitch is not accuracy, which the team cannot measure precisely at field level without labels. It is that USDA already holds the GR/SG field labels in FSA records, and this method would turn them into a national silage map on the RF-in-GEE stack NASS already runs. That claim is feasible, honest about its uncertainty, and fits the 40% presentation weight.
